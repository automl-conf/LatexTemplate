\subsection{Problem Setting}
\label{subsec:problem_setting}

In the single-device HW-NAS problem, the goal of a search strategy is to find the architecture \(h^* \in \mathcal{H}\) best balancing downstream task performance \( \text{Acc}(h), \ \forall h \in \mathcal H \) with task performance \( \text{Latency}(h) \ \forall h \in \mathcal H \), according to some weighting of choice.
Formally, 
\[
    \underset{h \in \mathcal{H}}{\max} \quad \text{Acc}(h) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \text{Latency}_d(h) \leq \tau,
\]
where \( \mathrm{Latency}_{d}(h) \) is the measured \emph{post-compilation} latency of architecture \( h \) on device \( d \), and \( \tau \) is an admissible latency threshold for that device. 
Our method extends this objective across a pool of diverse devices \( \mathcal D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots \} \), possibly each with a different \( \tau_i \).

We attain this framing HW-NAS as a sequential problem formalized through a Markov Decision Process (MDP). 
In the MDP, states \( s_t \) represents a given candidate architecture, and actions \( a_t \) correspond to modifying or finalizing the architecture. 
% In particular, we focus on cell-based NAS tackling NATS-Bench's topological searchspace~\citep{dong2021nats}.
In this context, states equate to candidate architectures' cells \( s \equiv [ o_1, o_2, \dots, o_5 ], \ o_i \in \mathcal O_{\text{NATS}} \). Actions \( a = [i, o_\text{new}], \ i=1, \dots, 6, \ o \in \mathcal O_\text{NATS} \) allow the controller to modify \( s \), selecting an operation \( o_i \in s \) to replace with \( o_\text{new} \).
The environment provides a scalar reward \( r_t = c_1 \pfreerea(s_t) + c_2 \phardware \), rewarding higher values of the considered TF-proxy, while guiding the policy to maximize hardware performance---in our case, minimizing latency.

In our method, the policy \( \pi \) is typically represented by a neural network itself, which takes as input states \( s_t \) and outputs actions \( a_t \). 
As our goal is to develop across-devices policies, \( s_t \) also contains information regarding candidates' latency. 
Figure~\ref{fig:policynet} shows the design for our policy network: taking as input \emph{(i)} representation of the candidate architecture \( h \) and \emph{(ii)} the latency of the candidate architecture the latency and  outputs a distribution over possible actions given the current architecture encoding $h(s_t)$. We adopt a trust-region or policy-gradient algorithm (e.g., PPO) to iteratively optimize $\theta$.
