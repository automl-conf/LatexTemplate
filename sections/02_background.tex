\subsection{Neural Architecture Search (NAS)}
Designing Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) by hand is cumbersome and brittle process, originally carried out by highly-specialized human experts, and later automatized via Neural Architecture Search (NAS).
Instead of relying on the combination of human ingenuity, heuristics, and trial-and-error to design DNNs, NAS provides an automated and systematic strategy to discover architectures optimizing a specified objective. 
In its seminal work,~\citet{zoph2016neural} demonstrated the feasibility of designing DNNs automatically via Reinforcement Learning (RL), using post-training performance metrics as reward signal.
While effective,~\citep{zoph2016neural}'s pioneering work most notably demanded thousands of GPU hours---a computational demand amply exceeding the capabilities of most institutions.
Subsequent research introduced more computationally efficient approaches, including weight-sharing paradigms~\citep{brock2017smash} and gradient-based methods~\citep{liu2018darts}.
A major goal of NAS is to reduce human intervention in selecting specialized architectures for novel tasks, data modalities, or deployment conditions. By systematically exploring a well-defined search space, NAS not only identifies high-accuracy networks but can also reveal unusual architectures that depart from human-engineered patterns. 
In the years following~\citet{zoph2016neural}'s work, NAS has expanded to address a variety of tasks within Deep Learning (DL), including language modeling, and sequence-to-sequence learning~\citep{elsken2019neural}. 
Despite these advances, the overall \emph{cost of search} remains a key concern, especially when architectures must be verified experimentally for downstream performance. In practice, it is common to search for architectures within cell-based searchspaces~\citep{zoph2018learning}, hereby reducing DNN design to identifying an optimal cell within a possibly very large searchspace of all possible combinations.

Under reasonable assumptions, the computational burden of NAS \(C \) can be modeled as the product of (i) the number of candidate architectures probed \( \#\text{networks}\), and (ii) the average \emph{cost to evaluate one network}, \( c \), yielding \( C = \sum_i^{\mathcal N} c_i \approx \vert \mathcal N \vert c \), for a pool of probed networks \(\mathcal N \). 
The cost of many early methods~\citep{zoph2016neural,baker2016designing,real2017large} was mainly dominated by \( c \), as they required \emph{training} candidate architectures on a target task to measure performance. Several strategies have been introduced to make evaluation more tractable. 
Training a single hypernetwork, for instance, amortizes \(C \) by training a single overparameterized model from which many sub-architectures can be sampled~\citep{brock2017smash, pham2018efficient,cai2019once}, hereby reducing \( \frac{\partial C}{\partial \vert \mathcal N \vert}\). However, training such a hypernetwork is still prohibitively expensive in scenarios where computational resources are not abundant, and does not have access to thousands of GPU hours~\citep{cai2019once} when searching large spaces of candidate architectures.

To tackle these limitations, the research community developed \emph{training-free metrics} (TF metrics)~\citep{mellor2021neural,jacot2018neural,chen2021neural,chen2023understanding}, which allow to drastically reduce the and cost and resources needed to evaluate candidate architectures by scoring them at initialization, without training.
Thus, TF metrics serve as proxies for post-training performance, guiding search toward promising architectures without requiring any update of any architecture parameters. In particular, in this work we focus on~\emph{NASWOT}~\citep{mellor2021nns}, which quantifies the expressivity of an untrained network by examining its weight initialization and the distinct activation patterns it can generate. By analyzing how the network divides input data---by measuring the number of "linear regions" formed by decision boundaries at initialization---NASWOT produces a one-shot score correlating with final performance. 
To search large spaces of candidate architectures, \citet{cavagnero2023freerea} propose complemeting NASWOT with \emph{LogSynflow}~\citep{tanaka2020synflow}, correlating the performance of a candidate design with the gradient flow of a loss function specifically designed to reflect the model's capacity.
Searching architectures using TF metrics substantially reduces the cost of probing each candidate architecture \( c \). In turn, TF metrics prove particularly useful to guide possibly sample-inefficient algorithms, such as RL~\citep{real2017large}, allowing RL to rival more sample-efficient methods like evolutionary algorithms~\citep{real2017large} for cell-based NAS.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning: Foundations and Applications}
Reinforcement learning (RL) is an area of machine learning concerned with training agents to act optimally in an environment that provides feedback via a reward signal \citep{sutton2018rl}. An RL agent observes states $s_t$, takes actions $a_t$, and receives rewards $r_t$ that reflect the desirability of those actions in light of the agent's goals. Over time, an RL agent attempts to learn a policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ that maximizes cumulative reward. Different algorithmic families have emerged, ranging from policy-gradient methods (such as Proximal Policy Optimization and Trust Region Policy Optimization) to value-based schemes (like Deep Q-Networks). RL has found applications in diverse domains, including robotics, resource allocation, and game-playing agents that exceed human performance \citep{mnih2015dqn, silver2017alpha}.

In the context of NAS, RL has historically been a method of choice for searching large, discrete architecture spaces. A controller network acts as the RL agent, sampling architectural configurations as actions and receiving a reward based on the candidate network's validation accuracy \citep{zoph2017nas, baker2016design}. However, unless augmented with advanced tricks such as early stopping, proxy objectives, or super-network weight sharing, RL-based NAS can be prohibitively expensive in scenarios that require training each architecture from scratch.

\subsection{From RL-based NAS to Alternative Methods}
While RL-based NAS set an important precedent, it was quickly recognized that repeatedly training every candidate network rendered the search protocol too costly. Researchers moved toward alternative solutions that attempt to reduce computational demand. Evolutionary algorithms \citep{real2019regularized} share conceptual similarities with RL controllers but can incorporate additional heuristics like tournament selection or age-based regularization. Gradient-based approaches, most notably Differentiable Architecture Search (DARTS) \citep{liu2019darts}, use continuous relaxations of the architecture space to enable gradient descent. Predictor-based methods \citep{wen2020neural}, in which a learned surrogate model estimates final accuracy from an architecture encoding, likewise offer a reduction in per-probe cost.

Despite these efforts, RL remains appealing for its flexibility and direct synergy with a reward signal that can encode multiple objectives beyond accuracy. Yet the primary challenge persists: how to reduce both the number of architectures tested and the per-architecture evaluation cost so that NAS becomes feasible at scale. This bottleneck is particularly evident when hardware constraints are introduced into the search.



\subsection{Hardware-Aware NAS: An Introduction}
While traditional NAS typically focuses on metrics such as validation accuracy or model size, hardware-aware NAS (HW-NAS) seeks to incorporate hardware performance indicators such as latency, memory usage, or energy consumption \citep{cai2019proxylessnas, wu2019fbnet}. This is crucial when deploying deep networks to edge devices and other constrained environments. An architecture that scores well on standard accuracy metrics might be infeasible due to unacceptable latency or resource overhead once deployed. Conversely, an architecture that runs efficiently on one device may underperform or fail real-time constraints on another.

Designing a comprehensive HW-NAS strategy is difficult for at least two reasons. First, measuring hardware performance frequently involves compiling and deploying each candidate to an actual device or specialized simulator, which can be costly. Second, performance profiles can vary across devices due to diverse hardware pipelines, compilers, and operator libraries, making it nontrivial to extrapolate latencies from one device to another. This leads to solutions that often specialize to one specific device or rely on approximate models of hardware costs.

\subsection{HW-NAS-Bench: A Standard for Hardware Evaluation}
HW-NAS-Bench \citep{li2021bench} was proposed to promote reproducibility and expedite research in hardware-aware design. Similar in spirit to NAS-Bench-101 or NAS-Bench-201 for accuracy evaluations, HW-NAS-Bench provides a curated set of architectures along with measured latency, energy, or other device metrics across multiple real-world hardware targets. By publishing a large table of (architecture, performance) tuples, it allows researchers to benchmark new search algorithms without the repeated overhead of measuring each candidate. Nonetheless, HW-NAS-Bench does not trivially extend to hardware settings not covered in its dataset, such as newly released edge accelerators or domain-specific hardware. This limitation, coupled with the cost of collecting new measurements, underscores the need for approaches that can adapt to unseen devices in a low-cost manner.

\subsection{Approaches to HW-NAS: OFA and HELP}
A variety of techniques tackle hardware constraints in NAS. \emph{Once-for-All (OFA)} \citep{cai2020once} trains a large super-network that is subsequently pruned or selected from to meet device-specific requirements. This approach effectively amortizes training costs by enabling quick extraction of sub-networks for different latency or memory budgets. However, OFA can be expensive to pre-train, and it requires carefully designed sampling strategies to ensure that every sub-network is well-trained and can generalize.

\emph{Hardware-adaptive Efficient Latent Predictor (HELP)} \citep{help2022} takes a different route by meta-learning a latency predictor that, once trained, can be rapidly queried for new architectures. Rather than repeatedly compiling candidate networks, HELP infers their latency through a learned surrogate. Although this reduces per-architecture probe time, it still demands an initial dataset of (architecture, latency) pairs for each hardware device of interest. As a result, the utility of HELP in highly diverse or previously unseen hardware scenarios may still be constrained by the up-front measurement cost.

\subsection{Domain Randomization in RL for Robotics}
Domain randomization (DR) is a popular technique in robotics and sim-to-real transfer, where the training environment is randomized across parameters such as friction, mass, sensor noise, or textures \citep{tobin2017domain, peng2018sim2real}. By exposing an RL agent to a broad distribution of simulated realities, DR encourages the agent's policy to exhibit robustness under real-world variability. DR has proven successful in tasks like robot grasping, where variations in object appearance are introduced in simulation, and the learned policy can adapt to actual sensor data without large additional tuning. This principle of “expose an agent to diverse synthetic conditions to improve generalization at test time” resonates with the need for cross-device adaptation in HW-NAS.

\subsection{Curriculum Learning and DORAEMON}
Curriculum learning \citep{bengio2009curriculum} proposes that agents learn more effectively when tasks are arranged in increasing order of difficulty. In the context of meta-learning or NAS, a curriculum may start with simpler search challenges (e.g., smaller architectures or smaller search spaces) before gradually introducing real-world complexity. DORAEMON \citep{doraemon2021} is one example of employing curriculum ideas to evolve meta-optimizers over progressively harder tasks. By gradually exposing a learning system to more challenging distributions, DORAEMON is able to craft meta-policies that exhibit resilient adaptation across varying conditions. 

For HW-NAS, a curriculum might involve starting with approximate or synthetic hardware metrics, then progressively including more realistic conditions, or introducing more complex architecture modifications.

\subsection{Comparisons to OFA and HELP: Our Angle}
Our proposed method diverges from OFA by circumventing the need to train a massive super-network. Instead, we aim to minimize overall measurements and training overhead by meta-learning a specialized RL controller that can adapt across different hardware devices without enumerating a super-network structure. This approach does not rely on a large volume of “shared” model weights, thus sidestepping the complexity of gating or pruning sub-networks.

Relative to HELP, which trains a meta-learned latency predictor for efficient inference, our approach aims to reduce the \emph{number} of networks probed at high fidelity, rather than the \emph{cost} of each probe. HELP still needs a nontrivial sampling of architectures to train the predictor, and any mismatch between predicted and real latency can degrade performance. By focusing on quickly narrowing down the candidates with an adaptive controller, our method avoids potential errors introduced by a learned latency model, while retaining the ability to test each candidate network in a direct (but limited) fashion on the hardware itself.
